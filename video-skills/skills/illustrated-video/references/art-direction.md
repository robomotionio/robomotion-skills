# Art direction

## The medium

Pick it once and hold it for the whole video. The default is **print**: a
paper stock with three to five spot inks, like a risograph or screen print.

- **Paper**: warm cream, cool white or newsprint. It is the white of the
  video: nothing is ever pure #FFFFFF.
- **Inks**: one dark (the line and text ink: navy, near-black, deep green),
  one or two loud (fluorescent pink, orange, red), one or two supporting
  (yellow, teal, sky). `PaperKit.INKSETS` has six tested sets; make your own
  from a brand's colours by keeping the same roles.
- **Tone** is halftone dots in an ink, never a gradient.
- **Grain** is multiplied over every frame, including the type.
- **Boil**: still linework re-draws a few times a second with a little
  jitter. It says "drawn by hand" more than any texture.
- **Registration**: hero type carries an offset shadow in a second ink,
  like a plate printed slightly off.

Other media work with the same kit when they fit the brief: newsprint
(black, red, one blue), blueprint (for technical products), a candy
palette (kids, food, playful consumer), noir (two inks and red). Decide by
the subject and audience, not by what looks impressive in isolation.

## Composition

- **Two sides.** When hero type is on screen, the type takes one side
  (usually left, where the eye starts) and the character or product the
  other. The background under the type is quiet: paper, a wash, a sparse
  grid. Busy sets (sunbursts, speed lines, tunnels) go behind shots with
  little or no type.
- **Scale contrast.** A frame reads when one thing is much bigger than
  everything else. Hero type at 150 to 300 px; a face that fills half the
  frame; a tiny figure in a huge space. Avoid everything-medium.
- **The bottom band** (y > 960) belongs to the subtitle chip whenever a line
  is playing.
- **Faces look into the frame**, toward the type or the thing they react to.

## Type tiers

| Tier | Kit | Use | Size |
|---|---|---|---|
| hero | `hero`, `heroStack`, `wordSlam` | The one to three words that carry the line: the title, the claim, the stressed word of a lyric | 150–320 px |
| mid | `mid` | An echo or aside in serif italic: the rest of a line, a whisper, a quote | 60–110 px |
| mono | `mono`, `label` | Counters, dates, file names, terminal lines, specs | 24–48 px |
| hand | `hand` | Marker lettering: reactions, sound effects, a scribbled note | 50–150 px |
| sub | `chip` | The subtitle: the whole line, the current word highlighted | 30–38 px |

Vary the presence: some lines are all hero, some only the chip, some a hero
word plus the chip. A video where every line gets the same treatment goes
flat within ten seconds. The opening is the loudest typographically.

## The hook: the first two seconds

The viewer decides in about two seconds. The first frame must already be a
designed image, not a fade from paper. Options that work:

- the first sung or spoken word as hero type, slammed on its syllable, with
  the protagonist's face on the other side, looking at the viewer;
- the product's promise in three words over its most striking screen;
- an extreme close-up (an eye, a hand, a logo) that pulls back on the
  first beat.

Put a title card with the name and a one-line tag in the first four
seconds, small (mono tier), in a corner, like a broadcast slate.

## Attention over the whole piece

Music videos that hold attention change something every one to two
seconds and repeat what works. Borrow the mechanics, not a style:

- **Cut on the music.** Bars and line starts for cuts; beats for hits
  inside a shot. In a chorus, cut faster; in a verse, hold longer shots.
- **Return to a home shot.** A set or framing that comes back each chorus or
  each section, changed a little each time, gives the video a shape.
- **An ensemble has a colour system.** Each member owns an ink (their
  clothes, their card), so a crowd reads at a glance and each has a name.
- **Direct address.** The protagonist looking into the lens on the key line
  is the strongest image you have. Save it for the lines that matter.
- **A clock that runs.** A date racing forward, a counter climbing, a
  meter filling: one device, always in the same corner, that makes the
  whole video feel like it is going somewhere.
- **Receipts.** Real specifics as paper inserts (a clipping, a label, a
  stamped form): they reward a second viewing and make the piece shareable.
- **Bookends.** The last shot rhymes with the first (the same framing, the
  same drawing, changed by what happened).

## Cultural references

When the subject lives in a community (a field, a fandom, an industry),
use the images that community already knows: its memes, charts, famous
papers, running jokes and screenshots. Recreate them in the video's inks;
do not paste them in. Name the thing correctly and use it for what it
means to that community. One precise reference beats five vague ones. If
you are not sure what a reference means, leave it out.

## What makes it look generated (avoid)

- a style that changes from shot to shot (a new palette, a new character
  design, photo next to flat drawing);
- stock "3D mascot" characters, glossy plastic, lens flares, purple-blue
  gradients, glassmorphism;
- every line of text the same size in the same place;
- text that repeats what the picture already says;
- symmetrical, centred, static compositions shot after shot;
- characters that stand and smile while nothing happens;
- cuts that ignore the music;
- invented facts, fake logos, fake quotes.
